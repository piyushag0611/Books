# The Rise and Fall of the Neoliberal Order — Key Takeaways

*A reflection on Gary Gerstle's landmark work on American political history*

---

## Introduction

Gary Gerstle's *The Rise and Fall of the Neoliberal Order* is one of the most illuminating books written about modern American politics. Gerstle argues that American political history is best understood not through individual elections or personalities, but through the rise and collapse of dominant "political orders" — cross-party consensuses that define the limits of legitimate politics for an entire generation. The New Deal order shaped American life from the 1930s to the 1970s. The neoliberal order — built on deregulated markets, shrinking government, and globalization — replaced it and dominated from Reagan through Obama. Today, that order is broken. Understanding how it rose, why it fell, and what replaced the forces that once kept it in check is essential for anyone trying to make sense of the world we now live in.

Two insights from this book have stayed with me above all others.

---

## Takeaway 1: Political Acquiescence — How Dominant Orders Absorb Their Opponents

The most striking sign that a political order has truly achieved dominance is not when its architects win elections — it is when the *opposing* party stops fighting it and begins governing within its assumptions.

Gerstle illustrates this with two near-identical moments separated by forty years.

When Dwight Eisenhower won the presidency in 1952, the Republican base expected him to dismantle Franklin Roosevelt's New Deal. Robert Taft — "Mr. Republican" — had campaigned on exactly that promise. Instead, Eisenhower accepted the welfare state, tolerated strong labor unions, and expanded Social Security. He did not just tolerate the New Deal order; he ratified it. The political calculus was clear: the New Deal had become so embedded in American life that attacking it was electoral poison. The order had won not just the government but the imagination of the country.

Four decades later, Bill Clinton performed the same act of acquiescence — this time toward the neoliberal order. After suffering a devastating defeat in the 1994 midterms, Clinton read the country's direction and moved decisively: NAFTA, welfare reform, the repeal of Glass-Steagall, balanced budget orthodoxy. He did not merely tolerate the neoliberal consensus — he became its most effective administrator, arguably more so than Reagan himself, because he neutralized the Democratic Party's resistance to it.

Clinton was not a pure opportunist. He had genuine intellectual convictions — shaped by the Democratic Leadership Council's argument that the New Deal coalition was structurally dead after five losses in six presidential elections. But those convictions bent toward whatever the moment made possible. His deeper drive was legacy and consequence, and the neoliberal order was the vehicle history offered him.

This is how political orders truly entrench themselves. Not through force, but through absorption — by making their assumptions so self-evident that even their opponents cannot imagine governing outside them.

---

## Takeaway 2: The Communist Effect — How the Cold War Protected Workers, and Its End Unleashed the Market

One of the most underappreciated arguments in the book is the paradoxical role the Soviet Union played in shaping American capitalism.

During the Cold War, the United States was engaged in a global contest not just of arms but of ideas. The central question — can capitalism deliver a better life than communism? — was never merely rhetorical. It had to be answered materially, in living standards, in security, in dignity. This competitive pressure created a structural incentive for American capitalism to be *generous*. Strong labor unions, a robust welfare state, genuine bargaining power for workers — these were not just the outcomes of progressive politics. They were also the price of ideological victory. You could not credibly argue that free markets outperformed central planning while your own workers lived in precarity.

The New Deal order, in this light, was partly held together by an external threat. Corporations tolerated redistribution. Politicians across both parties accepted the welfare state. The system had to prove itself.

Then 1989 happened. The Berlin Wall fell. The Soviet Union collapsed. And with it, the competitive pressure that had quietly disciplined American capitalism for half a century disappeared almost overnight.

The ideological floodgates opened. Francis Fukuyama declared the "End of History" — liberal democracy and free markets had won, and there was nothing left to prove. This was not just triumphalism. It was a permission structure. If markets had definitively won, why restrain them? The deregulation of finance, the acceleration of globalization, the hollowing out of labor protections — all of this gained enormous momentum in the 1990s, with the Soviet counterweight gone.

The cruel irony is this: communism's *existence* had protected workers under capitalism. Its *fall* hurt them.

---

## Closing Thought

What Gerstle gives us is not just history but a framework for reading the present. The neoliberal order is broken — cracked by the 2008 financial crisis, shattered by Trump and Sanders, hollowed out by decades of rising inequality and social atomization. But a broken order and a new one are not the same thing. We are living through the interregnum — the dangerous gap between what has ended and what has not yet begun. Understanding how we got here is the first step toward imagining something better.

*The Rise and Fall of the Neoliberal Order* is essential reading for anyone who wants to understand that journey.
