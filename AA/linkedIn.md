# Armageddon Averted: The Soviet Collapse, 1970-2000 — Key Takeaways

*A reflection on Stephen Kotkin's account of the most peaceful implosion of a superpower in history*

---

## Introduction

The fall of the Soviet Union in 1991 was not just the end of a superpower — it was the starting pistol for an era of unchecked market dominance in the West. It allowed Bill Clinton, the most efficient administrator of the neoliberal order, to bring in policies, repeal laws, and reduce the role of government in ways that even Reagan wasn't able to. This motivates anyone interested in history to grasp some basic understanding of the reasons behind the disintegration of the Soviet Union, the forces at play and how events unfolded from 1989, *fall of the Berlin Wall* to 1991, *Soviet disintegration and disbanding of the Communist Party*. Interestingly, Boris Yeltsin, then Russian President banned the party on Nov 6th, 1991, exactly 8 years later I was born, haha.

To my good luck, I found an amazing historian, Stephen Kotkin to describe the events that unfolded in 1989-1991. Stephen is currently a fellow at the Hoover Institution, and folks might have heard him with Peter Robinson in his show Uncommon Knowledge. He speaks eloquently and it is always a mesmerizing experience to hear him speak, I highly recommend his podcast with Dwarkesh Patel. Stephen has two books written on the disintegration of the Soviet and how the newly created Russian State was shaped by the Soviet remanants. His *Armageddon Averted* focuses on the core Soviet Union whereas *Uncivil Society* focuses on the Soviet Satellite states in Eastern Europe.

Armageddon Averted is a remarkably compact and lucid account of how the Soviet Union — a nuclear superpower armed to the teeth, with millions of loyal troops, a vast security apparatus, and seventy years of authoritarian infrastructure — simply ceased to exist.

The question Kotkin asks is not the one most people expect. He is less interested in *why* the Soviet Union collapsed than in **why it collapsed so peacefully.** No civil war. No nuclear incident. No military coup that held. No bloodbath remotely proportionate to the scale of what was dissolving. A superpower evaporated, and the world mostly watched on television. That, for Kotkin, is the real mystery — and his answer is both fascinating and deeply unsettling in its implications.

## Takeaway 1: The Party Was Not the Soviet Union's Ruler — It Was Its Skeleton

The standard image of the Communist Party is of a ruling class imposing its will on Soviet society from above. Kotkin's picture is more disturbing than that. The Party was not simply governing the Soviet Union. It *was* the Soviet Union — its connective tissue, its nervous system, its only real coordination mechanism.

Every significant appointment in the country — factory directors, newspaper editors, university rectors, military officers, local government officials — flowed through the Party's nomenklatura system. Judicial institutions, economic planning, social services, military command: none of these had ever developed genuine independence from Party structures, because they had never needed to. The Party had always been there, threading through everything.

Gorbachev understood the Party was the obstacle to reform. What he catastrophically underestimated was that the Party and the state were not separable — they were the same thing.

His moves were logical, even skilful in isolation. He created a new legislature with genuine competitive elections, giving the country an alternative source of political legitimacy. He removed Article 6 of the Soviet constitution — the clause that literally guaranteed the Party's monopoly on power. He built a Presidential office independent of the Party apparatus, intending to transfer authority to new institutions before the old ones collapsed. He refused to send tanks when Eastern Europe's communist regimes fell, signalling that reform was genuine.

Each move made sense. Together they pulled out the skeleton.

When the Party's authority collapsed, there was no functioning state beneath it to take over. No independent courts. No constitutional tradition. No civil society. No market. The institutions Gorbachev had built were too new and too shallow to fill a vacuum that deep. What followed — the chaos of the Yeltsin years, the oligarchs, the near-total collapse of state capacity — was not an accident. It was the direct consequence of removing the only thing holding a non-state together.

The Soviet Union collapsed peacefully in large part because the apparatus that might have fought to preserve it had already been quietly dismantled from within, by its own General Secretary, before anyone fully grasped what was happening.

---

## Takeaway 2: Glasnost and Perestroika — Reform as Unintentional Demolition

Gorbachev was not a closet liberal or a secret capitalist. He was a genuine believer in socialism who thought the Soviet project could be saved if the Party's stranglehold on information and economic life was loosened. He wanted to reform communism, not end it. That sincerity is what makes his story so tragic.

**Glasnost** — openness — was his instrument for breaking the information monopoly. By allowing criticism of the Party's history, starting with Stalin and expanding rapidly, he intended to generate public pressure for reform and restore faith in a system he believed could still work. What he unleashed instead was an avalanche. By 1987-88, Soviet newspapers were openly discussing the scale of the Gulag, the show trials, the purges. Millions of families discovered what had actually happened to relatives who had simply "disappeared." The Chernobyl disaster in 1986 — where Party officials had told people it was safe while children marched through contaminated air — had already cracked whatever remained of public trust. Glasnost turned that crack into a fracture.

**Perestroika** — restructuring — was his attempt to introduce limited market mechanisms into the Soviet economy without abandoning socialism. Factories were given autonomy from central planning. Private cooperative businesses were permitted for the first time since the 1920s. But price liberalization — the essential step that would have made any market mechanism functional — was repeatedly deferred because it meant devastating ordinary people's savings overnight. The result was the worst of both worlds: neither the coordination of the old central plan nor the incentives of a functioning market. Shortages worsened. Living standards fell.

The fatal interaction between the two was this: Gorbachev needed glasnost to build public pressure for perestroika — to overcome the Party apparatus's resistance to economic reform. But glasnost moved far faster than perestroika delivered results. By the time the economic reforms were visibly failing, he had already given people the freedom, the vocabulary, and the public platform to say so at full volume. He had created the critics before he had created the results that would have silenced them.

A man who genuinely believed in socialism, and genuinely wanted it to work for ordinary people, set in motion the forces that made both impossible.

## Closing Thought

Reading the book was a tad difficult largely because of the unfamiliarity with the Soviet Union and even now with Russia. India and Russia share a special relationship, but ask any Indian high schooler, to name 10 cities in the US vs 3 cities in Russia. Needless to say, I know all bets will be on the US. So, I had to spend some time with Claude to understand the governance and federal structure of the Soviet Union, the different power centers within the Communist Party, the Politburo and the Central Committee. Names are much more difficult to hold on to, anyway the book from Stephen Kotkin does really help in getting some basic idea and forming some kind of a mental picture of what was actually happening in the Soviet Union and the forces at play from 1988 to 1991.

As the Russian significance vaporises and the rivalry between the US and China takes center stage, I feel that if the Chinese would want to claim leadership of the world or they hit a potential brick wall (possibly due to falling birth rates), they will at some point have to reckon with loosening party control with some Chinese equivalent of *glasnost* and *perestroika*. It will be very interesting to see if any semblance of this happens within my life time. The Armageddon was averted in the Soviet case, I sincerely hope the Chinese system reforms rather than unravels to prevent such a situation. To get some more context on the Chinese system, and some history of the Chinese Communist Party, my next read is **Mao's Last Revolution** which is a record of the decade long Chinese Cultural Revolution.
