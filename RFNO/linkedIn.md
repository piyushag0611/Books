# The Rise and Fall of the Neoliberal Order — Key Takeaways

*A reflection on Gary Gerstle's landmark work on American political history*

---

## Introduction

Journaling and writing up a small note on books read especially around history, politics, economics has been on my agenda from the start of the year and I am glad I am able to kick it off now. I found Gary Gerstle's book "The Rise and Fall of the Neoliberal Order" from one of Ezra Klein's podcast (highly recommended), and found it as a very intriguing and easy to read and follow book. Gary Gerstle looks at American political history from the lens of the rise and collapse of dominant "political orders" — cross-party consensuses that define the limits of legitimate politics for an entire generation. Two such orders shaped the last century: the New Deal order, which built the American welfare state and ran from the 1930s to the 1970s, and the neoliberal order — built on deregulated markets, shrinking government, and globalization — which replaced it and dominated from Reagan through Obama. Today, the neoliberal order is unravelling across the Western world.

## Takeaway 1: Political Acquiescence — How Dominant Orders Absorb Their Opponents

The most striking sign that a political order has truly achieved dominance is not when its architects win elections — it is when the *opposing* party stops fighting it and begins governing within its assumptions.

Gerstle illustrates this with two near-identical moments separated by forty years. When Dwight Eisenhower won the presidency in 1952, the Republican base expected him to dismantle Roosevelt's New Deal. Instead, Eisenhower accepted the welfare state, tolerated strong labor unions, and expanded Social Security. He did not just tolerate the New Deal order — he ratified it. Four decades later, Bill Clinton performed the same act of acquiescence toward the neoliberal order. After a devastating defeat in the 1994 midterms, Clinton moved decisively: NAFTA, welfare reform, the repeal of Glass-Steagall, balanced budget orthodoxy. He did not merely tolerate the neoliberal consensus — he became its most effective administrator, arguably more so than Reagan himself, because he neutralized the Democratic Party's resistance to it.

This is how political orders truly entrench themselves. Not through force, but through absorption — by making their assumptions so self-evident that even their opponents cannot imagine governing outside them.

---

## Takeaway 2: The Communist Effect — How the Cold War Protected Workers, and Its End Unleashed the Market

One of the most underappreciated arguments in the book is the paradoxical role the Soviet Union played in shaping American capitalism.

During the Cold War, the United States was engaged in a global contest not just of arms but of ideas. The central question — can capitalism deliver a better life than communism? — had to be answered materially, in living standards, in security, in dignity. This competitive pressure created a structural incentive for American capitalism to be *generous*. Strong labor unions, a robust welfare state, genuine bargaining power for workers — these were not just the outcomes of progressive politics. They were also the price of ideological victory. You could not credibly argue that free markets outperformed central planning while your own workers lived in precarity.

Then 1989 happened. The Berlin Wall fell. The Soviet Union collapsed. And with it, the competitive pressure that had quietly disciplined American capitalism for half a century disappeared almost overnight. The ideological floodgates opened. Francis Fukuyama declared the "End of History" — liberal democracy and free markets had won, and there was nothing left to prove. This was not just triumphalism. It was a permission structure. If markets had definitively won, why restrain them? The deregulation of finance, the acceleration of globalization, the hollowing out of labor protections — all of this gained enormous momentum in the 1990s, with the Soviet counterweight gone.

The cruel irony is this: communism's *existence* had protected workers under capitalism. Its *fall* hurt them.

## Closing Thought

What Gerstle gives us is not just history but a framework for reading the present. The neoliberal order is broken — cracked by the 2008 financial crisis, shattered by Trump and Sanders, hollowed out by decades of rising inequality and social atomization. But a broken order and a new one are not the same thing. I believe we are in for a couple of decades of a Gilded age - rampant corruption, elite apathy toward ordinary lives, and social fabric too atomized to mount the kind of collective response to usher a new Progressive age. But I remain an ever hopeful on human ingenuity, that we will come together to build a better future in the next five decades, if not the next two.
